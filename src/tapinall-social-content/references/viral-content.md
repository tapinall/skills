# What makes content spread

Use this to break down why a post outperformed and to build drafts that can. The thresholds and signal rankings here come mostly from industry analyses rather than platform documentation: treat them as priors and let the brand's own data overrule them.

## Why people share

- **Arousal over valence.** Content that evokes high-arousal emotion, whether awe, anger or anxiety, is shared more; low-arousal emotion such as sadness is shared less. Positive content edges out negative overall (Berger & Milkman, 2012, about 7,000 news articles plus experiments). Aim for awe, amusement or useful urgency; leave outrage to brands that can afford the backlash.
- **STEPPS** (Berger, *Contagious*) as a scoring lens: Social currency (sharing makes the sharer look smart or in the know), Triggers (something in daily life brings it to mind), Emotion (high arousal), Public (visible use invites copying), Practical value (useful enough to send a friend), Stories (the message rides inside a narrative). A draft that hits none of the six is unlikely to travel.

## What each platform rewards, and what public data can show

Public data never includes watch time, so judge each platform by the proxy you can measure.

| Platform | Rewards (reported) | Observable proxy |
|---|---|---|
| TikTok | Watch time and completion, then shares, saves, rewatches | shares/views, saves/views |
| Instagram Reels | Watch time; sends via DM for reach beyond followers; saves | shares/views, saves/views |
| YouTube Shorts | Share of viewers who chose to watch rather than swipe away; average percentage viewed | views relative to subscribers and channel median |
| LinkedIn | Dwell time; substantive comments over reactions; carousels and documents hold dwell | comments/reactions, comment length |
| X | Replies, especially conversations the author joins; in X's 2023 open-source weights a reply counted 13.5 and a like 0.5 | replies/likes |

What platforms are reported to push down: engagement bait such as "comment YES" or "agree?" closers on LinkedIn, and reposts carrying another platform's watermark on Instagram.

## Breaking down an outlier

For each post scored 5× or more, record:

- Hook: its type and the exact first line or first three seconds.
- Format, length and structure.
- Dominant emotion and the STEPPS principles it hits.
- What it earned beyond views (shares, saves, comments) and what in the post asked for that.
- Transferable versus context-bound. A post carried by a celebrity, a news moment or a controversy teaches little the brand can reuse. A post that went viral through rage or clickbait is a pattern to avoid, not copy.

## Shared vocabulary

Hook types: result-first, contrarian, callout ("if you're a…"), open loop, numbered promise, mistake or stakes, story cold-open, visual pattern interrupt. Formats: talking head, voiceover b-roll, tutorial, listicle, carousel, meme, duet or stitch, text post, thread, long video. Use these tags in research and production alike so a gap's evidence maps straight onto its draft.

## Structure that holds attention

- Short video: the hook lands the promise or the result within 1–3 seconds and starts in motion. Give context fast, then reset attention every 8–12 seconds with a new visual, sound or question. Hold the payoff until late without burying it. Answering the open loop early ends the watch.
- Carousel: slide one is the hook, slide two gives the reason to keep swiping, and the last slide earns the save or share.
- Text post: the first line is the hook, because feeds cut posts off after a line or two. Write it to make the reader expand the post.
